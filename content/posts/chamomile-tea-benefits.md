Chamomile is probably the most familiar herbal tea in the world. It's made from the dried flowers of German chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*) or Roman chamomile (*Chamaemelum nobile*), and it has a soft, apple-like flavour that makes it an easy first herbal tea.

## What chamomile may help with

**Winding down.** Chamomile contains apigenin, a plant compound that binds to the same brain receptors as some calming medicines, though far more gently. Many people find a warm cup in the hour before bed is a useful part of a sleep routine. It isn't a sedative, and it won't fix insomnia on its own, but it can help with the ritual of slowing down.

**An unsettled stomach.** Chamomile has a long traditional use for mild indigestion, gas and cramping. Its mild antispasmodic effect is the likely reason a cup after a heavy meal feels good.

**A caffeine-free swap.** If you're cutting back on coffee or black tea in the afternoon, chamomile gives you a warm drink without anything that will keep you up.

## How to brew it

1. Use 1 tea bag or 1 heaped teaspoon of dried flowers per cup.
2. Pour over water just off the boil.
3. Cover the cup and steep for 5 minutes. Covering keeps the aromatic oils in.
4. Strain and drink plain, or add a little honey or a slice of lemon.

For a stronger "sleepy" blend, steep chamomile with a pinch of lavender or a few lemon balm leaves.

## Who should be careful

- **Ragweed or daisy-family allergies.** Chamomile is in the same plant family, so reactions are possible.
- **Blood thinners.** Chamomile may interact with warfarin and similar medicines. Ask your pharmacist.
- **Pregnancy.** Occasional cups are generally considered fine, but talk to your midwife or doctor before drinking it daily.

## The bottom line

Chamomile is a gentle, pleasant tea with a long track record. Enjoy it for comfort and routine rather than as a cure, and check with a professional if you take regular medication.
