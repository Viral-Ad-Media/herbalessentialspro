Stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*) grows almost everywhere, and most of us learned to avoid it as children. Drying or heating the leaves destroys the tiny stinging hairs, leaving a mild, green, nourishing herb.

## What does nettle tea taste like?

Grassy and earthy, a little like spinach or a mild green tea, but without the bitterness. It blends well with peppermint, lemon balm or a slice of lemon.

## Potential benefits

**Minerals.** Nettle leaves contain calcium, magnesium, iron and vitamin K. A cup of tea delivers a modest amount.

**Seasonal sniffles.** Nettle has a traditional use for hay fever, and a few small studies have looked at it for this purpose.

**A gentle diuretic.** Nettle encourages urine flow, which is one reason it appears in many "spring tonic" blends.

**Joint comfort.** Nettle has a long folk use for aching joints.

## How to make it

**Dried:** steep 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried leaf in boiling water for 5 to 10 minutes.

**Fresh:** wearing gloves, pick the top 4 to 6 leaves of young plants in spring, away from roadsides and sprayed areas. Rinse them, pour boiling water over a handful, and steep for 5 minutes.

For a stronger "overnight infusion", steep a heaped tablespoon in a covered jar of hot water for 4 to 8 hours, then strain.

## Who should be careful

Because it's a diuretic, check with your doctor if you take **diuretics, lithium, blood pressure or diabetes medicines**. Nettle's vitamin K content matters if you take **warfarin**. **Avoid it in pregnancy** unless your midwife approves.
